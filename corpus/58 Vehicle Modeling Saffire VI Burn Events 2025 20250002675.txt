Vehicle Modeling During the Burn Events in the Saffire VI 
Experiments 
Justin Niehaus1, John Brooker2, David Urban3, Gary Ruff4 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
 
In order to design a fire-safe environment inside a spacecraft, knowledge of the transport of heat and 
combustion products in a reduced gravity environment is needed. After a resupply mission to the ISS, the 
Saffire project provided large-scale fire data in a microgravity environment inside of Northrup Grumman’s 
Cygnus vehicle. Solid materials were burned, including a thin cotton-based sample and thick thermoplastic 
samples, to study a large-scale fire’s effect on a spacecraft. Sensors were placed in Cygnus that measured 
temperature and species concentration at four different standoffs, as well as nadir and zenith locations. 
Pyrosim was used to develop a model to predict the three burn events from Saffire VI using inlet and outlet 
data from the Saffire payload. The convective heat transfer coefficient between the cargo and the cabin air 
that was previously calibrated in the Saffire V model was used to predict the temperature response in the 
Saffire VI burn events. Good predictions of the CO2 and temperature responses for the remote sensors 
throughout Cygnus were realized.

I. Introduction 
ire events present some of the most catastrophic risks to a spacecraft. Previous programs such as Apollo and 
Skylab have undergone full-scale fire testing to help quantify this risk1-3. It is also standard practice in ground 
systems and submarines4,5. As planned oxygen levels in spacecraft begin to rise, it is important to have full-scale fire 
tests to help determine the spread of heat and combustion products. The Saffire project has provided such data in a 
microgravity environment. The sixth campaign, which took place on Northrop Grumman’s (NG) Cygnus vehicle 
after undocking from a resupply mission to the ISS, burned a cotton-based fabric, two Polymethyl (Methacrylate) 
(PMMA) samples and attempted to burn a Nomex fabric. The data collected from these tests, such as flame 
temperature and spread velocity will be useful to combustion scientists and fire-safety engineers in designing a safer 
spacecraft.  
The first three Saffire campaigns focused primarily on the large-scale flame spread inside the Saffire payload6-9, 
while the next three Saffire campaigns expanded the experiments to investigate the effect of a fire on the spacecraft. 
Sensors were placed throughout the vehicle to measured temperature and species concentration, such as CO2. These 
far-field gas and particle sensors will provide data that will increase understanding of smoke plume and combustion 
product transport within an orbiting vehicle10-13. In addition to the burn events, the test campaign includes flow 
visualization events, CO2 scrubs, and gas releases. Six remote sensors (RS) were utilized in Saffire IV-VI to 
measure temperature and product concentration at various locations in the cabin. A far-field device (FFD) was also 
placed in the zenith section, representing the cabin’s average temperature and species concentrations. All Saffire IV 
through VI campaigns used a cotton-based fuel and PMMA for the burn events. 
This paper presents the development of a model of the Cygnus vehicle during the Saffire VI experimental 
campaign and the simulation of the three burn events. The vehicle’s geometry and the trash and cargo placement 
after the resupply mission were determined from a map provided by NG, as well as photographs taken by the ISS 
crew. Inlet and outlet sensor data from the Saffire payload was used to calculate the heat input and species 
generation rate from Saffire to the Cygnus vehicle. The effective convective heat transfer between the cargo and the 
cabin air that was calibrated in the work describing model development of Saffire V is also used in this work14. The 
primary verification mechanism of the model was temperature and CO2 data provided at the RS locations. 
Developing verified models using full-scale fire tests is an excellent way to gain knowledge of the unknown and 
move towards a safer environment in space. 
II. Saffire Experiment 
The Saffire campaign of experiments has taken place on the NG Cygnus vehicle during a resupply mission to the 
ISS. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the Saffire experiment within the Cygnus vehicle without the trash and cargo 
that form standoffs for heat and combustion product transfer. Vital instrumentation and the vehicle Environmental 
Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) are illustrated. The size of the pressure vessel is nearly 3 meters in 
diameter by 4 meters in length.

In between the arrival and departure of the Cygnus vehicle from the ISS, trash and cargo were placed to make a 
free air volume that would allow for control of the oxygen concentration when O2 bottles are released in between 
specified events in the campaign. The placement of the cargo, shown in Figure 2, creates channels or standoffs that 
transport heat and smoke from the Saffire payload at the nadir end of the vehicle to the zenith end of the vehicle. In 
the model, a mass generation rate (MGR) and a heat addition rate (HAR) are applied to the Saffire outlet, which has 
a surface area of 50 cm2, to apply heat and combustion products into the domain.

Nine events occurred during the Saffire VI experimental campaign, including the three burn events that are 
simulated in this work. These events are described in Table 1. Similar to previous Saffire campaigns, Saffire VI 
started with pulling cabin air through the FFD to scrub the vehicle of CO2. A scrub was also performed during event 
7, between the two PMMA burn events. During event 2, CO2 was released into the vehicle to calculate the free 
volume in the cabin and aid in analyzing the O2 that would need to be released during the burn events. HCl was also 
released during event 2 to study acid gas adsorption to surfaces. After all the planned burns were completed, a flow 
visualization test was performed during 9 to determine any major differences in the Saffire Flow Unit (SFU) from 
event 3. An attempt to burn Nomex occurred during event 4, but no ignition was successful. Event 5  burned SIBAL 
fabric at 31.0% O2 and is the first event simulated in this work. This work also simulates the two-sided PMMA burn 
and one-sided PMMA burn in events 6 and 8, respectively. The initial atmospheric conditions for the experimental 
campaign at the start of the burn events were 54.1 kPA and 31.0% oxygen, with a temperature of approximately 
24.3°C. 
III. Modeling 
A model was developed to predict the effect of the three burn events of the Cygnus vehicle by utilizing the Fire 
Dynamics Simulator (FDS), a code developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). This 
code is optimized for large-eddy turbulent simulations and for low-speed flows usually found in compartment 
fires17. The graphical interface that was used to develop the vehicle model is Pyrosim TM18. Figure 2 provides a 
picture of this model, highlighting the important flow boundary conditions and sensors. This geometry was

comprised of a cargo placement map and photographs provided by NG. The mesh size was 0.05 m. A mesh 
sensitivity analysis was completed to determine the effect of mesh size on accuracy of results. 
The model was primarily compared to the experiments using the CO2 and temperature readings at the locations 
of the six remote sensors. All six of these sensors can be seen in Figure 1, while the cargo hides RS2 and RS5 in 
Figure 2, which has also been described in detail in previous papers about Saffire IV and V14-16. The initial 
temperature of the cabin used for each simulation was the initial temperature of RS3 since that was closest to the 
Saffire outlet, which created the greatest response for comparison. Since the initial temperature of all the remote 
sensors was different in the experiment, the other remote sensor simulation curves were adjusted after the simulation 
to match the initial temperature of the experimental curves. 
 
The flow rates used for the boundary conditions in the simulations are provided in Table 2, which are provided 
experimentally. The SFU used a flow rate of 0.028 m3/s to give a flow of approximately 20 cm/s over the samples 
during times of burning but was ramped down at certain times to promote flame extinguishment. The heat from the 
burn event is captured at the outlet of the Cygnus vehicle by the HAR, which is calculated using the inlet and outlet 
temperature of the SFU. The HAR is calculated using Eq. 1 and demonstrated in Figure 3 using data from event 5. 
Since no flame or combustion properties are inputted into the model, the SFU simply acts as an object that intakes 
cool cabin air and outputs combustion products and hot air. Radiation from the SFU has been assumed to be 
negligible. The initial atmospheric conditions of the Cygnus vehicle are shown in Table 3. The density and specific 
heat of the air used to calculate the HAR are taken at the initial temperature since the outlet of the SFU does not 
exceed 75°C during any burn. Density and specific heat would also be negligibly affected by the increase in main 
combustion products CO2 and H2O in the atmosphere, which reaches no more than 2% during any burn event.

IV. Results and Discussion 
 The initial experimental RS CO2 values for the SIBAL burn event ranged from 5,078 to 5,704 ppm, giving an 
average of 5,381 ppm as given by Table 3. This average value was used for the initial cabin CO2 concentration for 
the event 5 simulation, and then the prediction curves were increased or decreased after the simulation to match the 
initial experimental values. This method was used consistently for event 6 and 8 as well. Figure 6 shows the 
experimental CO2 results at the six RS locations against the simulated results. An excellent agreement is realized 
once the curves reached steady state. This is expected since the CO2 added to the cabin from the MGR (46.5 g) was 
close to the average CO2 rise predicted from the six remote sensors (46.8 g). However, the rise in the curve is 
underpredicted by the model at all locations. The largest overshoot of the steady state was RS3 and RS6, which were 
in the direct path of the Saffire outlet flow. RS2 also had a considerable overshoot in the experimental curve, which 
was the RS below RS3, indicating that the flow was able to be diverted down into the starboard standoff. It is clear 
the total amount of CO2 being delivered into the cabin from Saffire is accurate, but how much reaches various parts 
of the cabin early on during the burn event is inaccurate. If this was a consistent problem among all three burn 
events, a reasonable conclusion would be that the cargo in the model, especially at the FP standoff needs to be 
adjusted for more flow to go over RS3. However, as will be discussed later, the CO2 predictions are better during 
event 6 and 8.

The temperature prediction during the SIBAL burn at the six RS locations are shown in Figure 7. The same 
convective heat transfer coefficient of 150 W/m2-K that was used for the cargo surfaces in the Saffire V model was 
used for this work14,15. Since the remote sensors ranged from 19.9 to 24.3 °C initially, the prediction curves were 
adjusted so that the initial value matched that of each location to compare the temperature increase. This method was 
also used for events 6 and 8 as well. The prediction given by the model is in excellent agreement with the 
experimental model. Besides RS3 and RS6, which are the two in the most direct path of the Saffire outlet flow, there 
was negligible increase in temperature observed during the burn event compared to the sensitivity of the 
thermocouples (+/- 0.25 °C). The model predicted this behavior. The total amount of heat input into the Cygnus 
cabin from Saffire was 124 kJ, which can be calculated by integrating the HAR provided by Eq.1 and Figure 4. This 
agrees well with the cotton sample in Saffire V that provided 110 kJ of heat into the Cygnus vehicle from the 
payload.

Figure 8 shows the CO2 RS predictions for the two-sided PMMA burn during event 6. Excellent agreement 
between prediction and experimental data during the ramp-up phase and steady-state part of the curve is realized for 
all RS locations. Unlike the SIBAL burn, the experimental curves did not overshoot the steady state quite as much, 
which was predicted well in the model. The average increase in the RS data was 8164 ppm, representing a 128.1 g 
increase in CO2. This matches well with the total input from the MGR of 127.8 g. 
 
The predictions for RS temperature response during the two-sided PMMA burn are provided in Figure 9. As 
discussed above, only RS3 and RS6, which are in the direct path of the Saffire outlet flow, are shown here since they 
are the only locations that register a temperature response. A good agreement can be seen during the ramp-up phase 
from RS3 and RS6. However, the model does overpredict the maximum temperature. A slight overprediction in 
temperature rise is realized during the SIBAL burn, but it is less noticeable due to the lower heat input. Other 
surfaces besides the trash and cargo are set to be adiabatic in the model, but could be contributing extra heat loss 
(rails, straps, etc.). Integrating the heat addition rate for this event gave a total energy addition of 497 kJ. This is 
significantly higher than the 233 kJ calculated by a similarly sized PMMA burned during Saffire V. This is likely 
attributed to the higher O2 level in Saffire VI (30.3%) as compared to Saffire V (26.5%).

Figure 10 shows the CO2 RS predictions for the one-sided PMMA burn during event 8. This simulation provides 
the best prediction out of the three burn events, which gives added evidence that the bulk cargo placement and air 
flow within the model are well represented. The average increase in the RS data was 8229 ppm, representing a 130.3

Figure 10. Saffire VI CO2  RS response curves during one-sided PMMA burn test (event 8). 
 
During the two PMMA burn events, a dip in CO2 concentration is observed at the RS2 location after the ramp up 
before quickly recovering. This is not captured by the model. One explanation for this dip is that RS2 is close to the 
ECLSS outlet, which cycles on and off in an attempt to keep the conditions of Cygnus within certain parameters.  
RS3 is also close to the ECLSS outlet, but also close to the hot gas coming from the Saffire outlet, which likely 
dictates its behavior. RS2 can be reasonably assumed to be more influenced by slight changes in vehicle flow 
patterns. 
The temperature RS predictions for the one-sided PMMA burn during event 8 are presented in Figure 11. An 
excellent agreement is realized for RS3, while an over-prediction is realized for RS6. The experimental data for RS6 
appears nonresponsive, similar to the other four locations. While it can be explained why the heat from the fire does 
not reach those locations, RS6 has been responsive in the previous two burn events, as well as burn events in the 
previous two Saffire campaigns14,15,16. This indicates that RS6 might not be responding properly during the burn 
event.

These large-scale experiments and numerical models highlighted the effect a spacecraft fire has on a vehicle, 
such as heat transfer and product mixing. The results from this work demonstrate the feasibility of predicting a fire’s 
impact on a spacecraft’s habitability. These verified models from Saffire IV to Saffire VI can be used to predict 
“worst-case” fire scenarios. For example, heat release rates from experimental calorimetry experiments, such as Li-
ion batteries, could be provided as input to the model, or the gravity level could be adjusted to study the buoyant 
effects of heat transfer inside a vehicle under lunar or Martian gravity.  
 
V. Conclusions 
 
The effects of the Saffire burn events on the Cygnus vehicle were revealed using a model developed using FDS. 
Inlet and outlet sensors to the Saffire payload served as the main inputs into the model, while six remote sensors 
placed throughout the cabin served as the verification of the model. In previous work, an effective convective heat 
transfer coefficient of 150 W/m2-K for the cargo in the standoffs that was calibrated at RS3 for the Saffire V cotton 
burn was used for the three simulations in this work and provided adequate temperature predictions in the cabin. 
Besides a couple of locations during specific burn events, the model predicted CO2 and temperature throughout the 
cabin well. These results, and the simulations of the other Saffire campaigns demonstrate the feasibility of predicting 
a fire’s impact on the habitability of an orbiting spacecraft. With validated models in place, other fire sources, such 
as Li-ion batteries, can be used to determine the effect of that type of fire in a spacecraft. Similar methods of 
modeling could also be incorporated with other spacecrafts and gravity levels.
