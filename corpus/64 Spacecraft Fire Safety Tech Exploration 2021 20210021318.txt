Conclusions
♦We have made progress to address the exploration capability gaps identified by the SCLT
•
Material flammability and flammability limits in Lunar-g is a high priority – especially because of the 
elevated %O2
♦Owing to lack of access to partial gravity test platforms, the data set of lunar flammability 
tests is very small
♦Theory and experimental results indicate that oxygen flammability limits in lunar-g are 
lower than those measured in 1-g
•
The magnitude of ∆%O2 depends not only on the material but on the direction of burning (concurrent or 
opposed)
♦The planned habitat atmosphere exacerbates these issues by enhancing the flammability
♦Additional work is needed to establish a partial gravity flammability acceptance criterion
♦Lunar gravity can change fire detection strategy depending on vehicle design
♦Turning off forced air flow cannot be used as an extinguishment strategy
♦Work on post-fire clean-up and emergency mask is progressing following an update to the 
sorbent packing procedure

Fire Safety Gaps
15
Gap Number
Title
Description
2021-2900-​TX06 
On board monitors that 
measure particulates in the 
crew respiratory hazard range, 
survive dormancy, and work in 
low pressures. 
There are currently three types of particulates that must be both 
individually quantified and identified during Exploration missions: 
smoke, cabin particulates, and planetary dust. This needs to 
be demonstrated in both 14.5 psia and reduced pressure (8.2 psia). 
There are no Commercial Off the Shelf (COTs) technologies that yet 
provide this suite of capabilities for infusion. Currently habitat 
maintenance (like vacuum cleaner use or rack rotations) can release 
particles and cause false fire alarms. Consequently smoke alarms are 
often disabled during maintenance causing a gap in fire 
detection. Particle monitors allows you to distinguish between fire 
particles, planetary dust, and crew dust to understand and reduce 
the impact of false fire alarms.

Fire Safety Gaps
16
Gap Number
Title
Description
2021-2906-​TX06
Clothing for 
enriched oxygen 
atmospheres 
Current crew clothing is flammable in the 21% O2 (14.7 psia) atmosphere of 
ISS. Future vehicles will be operated at lower pressures and corresponding 
higher O2 (30% O2 at 10.2 psia and 36% O2 at 8.3psia). Clothing becomes even 
more of a fire hazard under these elevated O2 conditions. For the safety of the 
crew, clothing that will not ignite under nominal atmospheric conditions is 
needed. 
2021-2913-​TX06
Fire Safety Training 
and Facilities
Emergency equipment is designed as a last resort when all other engineering 
controls fail. Prior to flight, crews are trained on the ground in the procedures 
needed to respond to emergencies, particularly fire emergencies. Historical 
incidents have shown the dangers of incomplete training in emergency scenarios. 
Prior to flight, US astronauts are never provided an opportunity to exercise the 
equipment or to practice the emergency response scenario while in the confines 
of the vehicle. This introduces a well-documented risk to the crews’ ability to 
quickly and accurately respond to a fire emergency. A mandatory training 
program and a test area with realistic facilities is needed to ensure crew 
familiarity with fire response protocols and experience with fire response 
hardware in the tight confines of spacecraft.​

2021-2934-​TX06
Material flammability 
and fire propagation 
in low- and partial-
gravity 
environments, and at 
exploration 
atmospheres
Material flammability and fire propagation in 1-g, atmospheric pressure, and 
atmospheric oxygen environments are well characterized due to readily 
available terrestrial data. Very limited data is available for flammability of 
materials or fire propagation in low- and partial-gravity environments. 
Additional oxygen compatibility data is needed for common cabin materials at 
exploration vehicle cabin pressures and oxygen concentrations (e.g. 10.2 psia 
total pressure/26.5% oxygen concentration and 8.2 psia total pressure/34% +/-
2%. oxygen concentration.) Historically, NASA has used 1-g testing to try to 
extrapolate and predict the flammability and propagation in these 
environments, which increases the overall fire risk in space missions since it 
directly informs flammability limits, fire detection, fire suppression, fire 
response, and post-fire clean-up.
2021-2935-​TX06
Fire Detection, Fire 
Suppression, and 
Post-Fire Monitoring 
and Clean-up
Due to the inherent danger of fires in closed environments, fire detection, fire 
suppression, and post-fire monitoring and clean-up will be provided on all 
vehicles. ISS, commercial vehicles, and Orion have all developed solutions for 
meeting these requirements. For future missions, this suite of solutions will be 
evaluated for each individual vehicle architecture to determine the best 
approach to fire detection, suppression, and clean-up.

Fire Safety Gaps

Gap Number

2021-2964-​TX06
Fire emergency 
breathing mask
During fire emergencies, the crew don a portable breathing apparatus which 
provides clean breathing oxygen either by filtering out toxic gases produced 
from a fire and/or provides O2 to the crew from stored oxygen sources or by 
conversion from other gases. The device type and mechanism chosen must be 
compatible with mission architecture (i.e. suppressants used, oxygen availability, 
expected type and amount of environmental toxins, etc). The breathing 
apparatus currently used on ISS is not incompatible with Orion architecture. In 
Orion, due the small volume, O2 provided to the crew would result in an 
increase in vehicle O2 levels, thereby further increasing fire risk. Additionally, 
the Orion vehicle uses a water mist fire extinguisher. Flow through the filter of 
the ISS breathing mask may be blocked by water filming across the filter surface. 
A new design for fire emergency breathing mask is needed to meet the fire 
safety architecture design for Orion and possibly future vehicles.
