# 3.4 Suppression agents
**Module:** 3 — Fire Detection & Suppression · **Prev:** [3.3](3.3-diagnostics-sensors.md) · **Next:** [3.5](3.5-extinguishment-effectiveness.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Spacecraft carry specific extinguishing agents, each targeting one side of the fire tetrahedron.
- Shuttle used Halon 1301; ISS modules use CO₂ (Russian cabins: foam; decompression chamber: N₂). `[14, 28]`
- Each agent removes a side of the fire tetrahedron (heat, oxidizer, or chain reaction). `[28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
An extinguishing agent works by attacking one side of the fire tetrahedron (Lesson 1.2). Water cools (removes heat). CO₂ and N₂ smother (displace oxygen). Halon 1301 chemically inhibits (breaks the chain reaction). Different spacecraft use different agents for different reasons.
- Halon 1301 (chemical, breaks chain reaction) — used on Shuttle. `[14, 28]`
- CO₂ (inerting, removes oxidizer) — used in ISS modules. `[28]`
- Russian cabins: mixed-phase foam; decompression chamber: nitrogen. `[28]`
- Extinguishers are carried on all crewed spacecraft but rarely used (5 minor overheat events, none flaming). `[28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The choice of agent is a real engineering trade-off. Halon 1301 is remarkably effective (chemical inhibition at low concentration) but depletes ozone, so it's being phased out. CO₂ smothers fire but is toxic at the concentrations needed. Foam cools and seals but is messy and heavy. Nitrogen is inert but needs a sealed, low-oxygen environment to work. In a sealed spacecraft, you can't just "open a window" — so the agent must be effective, non-toxic, and leave the air breathable afterward. That tension is why the hunt for a Halon replacement (Lesson 3.6) is an active research area, and why suppression in space is a design problem, not a one-size-fits-all answer.
