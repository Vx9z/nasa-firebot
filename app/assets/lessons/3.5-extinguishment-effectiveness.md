# 3.5 Extinguishment effectiveness
**Module:** 3 — Fire Detection & Suppression · **Prev:** [3.4](3.4-suppression-agents.md) · **Next:** [3.6](3.6-halon-replacement.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
How we measure and compare how good an agent is at putting out a fire.
- Cup-burner flame extinguishment quantifies how much agent is needed. `[21]`
- FPDS (Fire Prevention, Detection & Suppression) is the programmatic umbrella. `[30]`

## Level 2 — Detail
"Effectiveness" means: how much agent does it take to put out a standard flame? The cup-burner test answers this — a standard flame is exposed to an increasing concentration of agent until it goes out, giving a minimum extinguishing concentration. That lets engineers compare agents apples-to-apples.
- The cup-burner test measures the minimum extinguishing concentration of an agent. `[21]`
- FPDS organizes fire prevention, detection, and suppression R&D for exploration. `[30]`
- Standardized suppression-system design would benefit from available research data. `[28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The cup-burner is the classic benchmark: a small cup of fuel burns in a controlled air stream, and the agent is added until the flame dies. The result — the minimum extinguishing concentration — is a single number that captures an agent's potency, and it's how regulators and engineers compare water mist, CO₂, Halon, and candidates. But "effectiveness" in a cup-burner isn't the whole story: an agent must also be non-toxic, clean, and leave breathable air. FPDS bundles all of this — prevention, detection, suppression — into one program because a spacecraft fire-safety system is judged as a whole, not by any single number.
