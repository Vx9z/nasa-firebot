# 2.4 Droplet combustion (FLEX)
**Module:** 2 — Microgravity Combustion · **Prev:** [2.3](2.3-flame-spread-microgravity.md) · **Next:** [2.5](2.5-gas-phase-flames.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Microgravity lets us study the "ideal" fire: a single, perfectly spherical burning droplet.
- µg permits near-spherical droplets (2–6 mm) for idealized study. `[14]`
- Droplets form a soot shell + concentric flame; bi-component fuels burn disruptively. `[14, 20]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A burning liquid droplet is the simplest possible fire — and it's almost impossible to study on Earth, where buoyancy distorts it. In microgravity, a droplet hangs as a near-perfect sphere, surrounded by a concentric spherical flame and a shell of soot. This lets researchers measure burning rates and soot formation with clean, idealized geometry.
- Spherical droplet → concentric spherical flame + soot shell (idealized geometry). `[14, 20]`
- Droplets up to 2–6 mm are stable in µg (too large to study on Earth due to buoyancy). `[14]`
- Bi-component fuels with very different volatility burn disruptively (near-explosive). `[14]`
- FLEX-2J: JAXA collaboration extended FLEX to droplet arrays. `[17]`
- Supercritical droplet combustion informs high-pressure combustor design (rocket, aircraft). `[14]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The FLEX (Flame Extinguishment Experiment) program ran on the ISS using the Multi-User Droplet Combustion Apparatus, and it's a perfect example of why microgravity matters. On Earth you can only study tiny droplets (~0.5 mm) because bigger ones sag and deform under buoyancy. In space you can levitate droplets up to 6 mm and watch them burn as clean spheres — revealing phenomena like the soot shell, disruptive burning of mixed fuels, and supercritical combustion that are hidden on the ground. Those findings feed directly into fuel-spray and engine design, closing the loop from "idealized space experiment" back to "better rockets and aircraft."
