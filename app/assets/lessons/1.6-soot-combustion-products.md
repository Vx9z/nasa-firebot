# 1.6 Soot & combustion products
**Module:** 1 — Combustion & Fire · **Prev:** [1.5](1.5-extinction-blowoff.md) · **Next:** [2.1](2.1-what-changes-microgravity.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Incomplete combustion makes soot and other products — and soot is gravity-sensitive.
- Soot = carbon-rich particles from incomplete combustion; makes flames yellow/luminous.  `[14]`
- Products include CO₂, CO, water, soot, unburned hydrocarbons, and toxic species. `[14, 28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
When combustion is incomplete, carbon clumps into tiny solid particles — soot. Soot is what makes a flame glow yellow; a clean, soot-free flame burns blue. Along with soot, combustion produces gases: CO₂ and water (complete), plus CO, unburned fuel, and other species (incomplete).
- Soot forms by nucleation, surface growth, then agglomeration of carbon particles. `[14]`
- Soot makes flames yellow and luminous; soot-free flames burn blue (cleaner, cooler). `[14]`
- In µg, soot agglomeration differs — longer residence time, no buoyant removal. `[14, 20]`
- Carbon monoxide (CO) is the principal toxic threat from incomplete combustion. `[28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Soot is the link between "how a flame looks" and "how dangerous it is." Yellow = soot = incomplete, inefficient, smoky, and strongly radiating; blue = cleaner. In microgravity, soot behaves very differently: particles linger longer (no buoyant plume to carry them away) and agglomerate into distinctive structures — which changes both flame appearance and the smoke a detector sees (Module 3). Soot also makes flames radiate strongly, tying directly to radiative extinction (Lesson 1.5). So soot isn't a side detail — it sits at the intersection of flame color, temperature, detection, and toxicity.
