# 2.5 Gas-phase flames (ACME)
**Module:** 2 — Microgravity Combustion · **Prev:** [2.4](2.4-droplet-combustion.md) · **Next:** [2.6](2.6-solid-fuel-combustion.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Gaseous fuel flames — the ACME program and jet flames — reveal microgravity flame physics.
- ACME studied gaseous flames on ISS (multiple sub-experiments). `[23, 24]`
- Multi-jet flames (China Space Station): flame length grows with fuel velocity, exceeds theory via jet interaction. `[27]`

## Level 2 — Detail
Gaseous flames — jets, coflow, spherical — are the workhorses of microgravity combustion research because they're clean and controllable. ACME ran several sub-experiments on ISS, and the China Space Station's multi-jet study added new data on how neighboring jets interact.
- ACME ran several gas-flame sub-experiments (spherical flames, coflow, electric-field effects). `[23, 24]`
- China multi-jet: flame length grows monotonically with fuel velocity; jet interaction makes it exceed theory. `[27]`
- N₂ dilution brightens flames (soot confinement); O₂ enrichment changes growth rate differently. `[27]`
- Oxygen/fuel jet flames are motivated by spacecraft oxygen-storage fire safety. `[22]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Gas-phase flames are where microgravity research produces the cleanest, most quantitative data. ACME measured things like how flames respond to electric fields and how they burn in coflow — measurements that would be swamped by buoyancy on Earth. The China Space Station work went further, looking at *multi-jet* interactions: when jets burn side by side, they merge and stretch flames beyond what single-jet theory predicts, and dilution (N₂) vs. oxygen-enrichment change flame length in very different ways. This matters directly for fire safety around oxygen systems — a leaking O₂ line feeding a jet flame is a real spacecraft scenario (hence the oxygen/fuel jet studies).
