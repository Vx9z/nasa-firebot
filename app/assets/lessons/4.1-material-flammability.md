# 4.1 Material flammability
**Module:** 4 — Materials, Toxicity & Standards · **Prev:** [3.6](3.6-halon-replacement.md) · **Next:** [4.2](4.2-toxic-hazardous-products.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Which materials burn, how easily, and why it differs in space.
- Spacecraft polymers can generate acids, ammonia, and other toxic products. `[28]`
- PMMA flammability depends on thickness, preheat, and buoyancy. `[33, 34]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A spacecraft is full of flammable solids — wire insulation, panels, fabrics, cargo. Whether they ignite and how fast they burn depends on the material itself (composition, thickness) and the conditions (preheat, airflow, gravity). The same material can behave very differently in microgravity than on Earth.
- Flammability varies with thickness, preheat, flow, and material composition. `[33, 34]`
- PTFE (wire insulation) is a key concern — it degrades to toxic products when overheated. `[14]`
- Silicone sample flammability depends on buoyant conditions. `[45]`
- Materials flammability research is continuously updated for spacecraft. `[62]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Material flammability is the *prevention* half of fire safety (Lesson 5.2). If you can choose materials that don't easily ignite or spread flame, you've removed the fuel side of the tetrahedron before any fire starts. The catch is that microgravity changes the rules: without buoyant convection carrying heat away, some materials ignite and spread more readily than their Earth tests suggest. PTFE is the classic worry — it's everywhere as wire insulation, but overheated it breaks down into toxic gases and ultrafine particles. So spacecraft material selection isn't just "does it burn?" — it's "does it burn *in microgravity*, and what does it release when it does?"
