# 0.6 Gravity & the microgravity environment
**Module:** 0 — Foundations · **Prev:** [0.5](0.5-heat-transfer.md) · **Next:** [1.1](1.1-what-is-combustion.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
"Microgravity" is near-weightlessness (free-fall), not zero gravity.
- Microgravity = free-fall, not "zero" gravity. 
- Produced by drop towers, parabolic aircraft, sounding rockets, and orbit. `[14]`

## Level 2 — Detail
Weight is the force of gravity on mass. In free-fall — an orbit, a falling drop tower capsule, a parabolic "vomit comet" dive — everything falls together, so objects feel weightless. That's microgravity, and it's how we study combustion without buoyancy.
- "Zero-g" is really microgravity (~10⁻⁶ g) — residual accelerations from drag, g-jitter, etc. 
- Facilities by duration: drop towers (~seconds), parabolic flights (~20–30 s), sounding rockets (~minutes), orbit (continuous). `[14]`
- An orbiting spacecraft is in continuous free-fall around Earth → sustained microgravity. 
- Drop-tower and space experiments need small, rugged, fast-responding instruments. `[14]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Each facility trades *duration* for *quality*. Drop towers give seconds of high-quality µg cheaply and repeatedly. Parabolic flights give ~20–30 s in bursts. Sounding rockets give minutes. The ISS gives continuous µg for weeks — which is why the big experiments (FLEX, ACME, BASS, SoFIE, Saffire) flew there. Understanding this trade-off explains *why* the literature has both quick drop-tower studies and long-duration ISS campaigns, and why some instruments must survive violent deceleration on recovery.
