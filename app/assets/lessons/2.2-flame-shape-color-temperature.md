# 2.2 Flame shape, color & temperature
**Module:** 2 — Microgravity Combustion · **Prev:** [2.1](2.1-what-changes-microgravity.md) · **Next:** [2.3](2.3-flame-spread-microgravity.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Space flames look very different: spherical, dimmer, bluer, and cooler.
- µg flames are spherical/dome-shaped, dimmer, bluer (less soot), and cooler. `[14, 27, 38]`
- Pure methane burns dimmer (lower temp); N₂-diluted flames brighten via soot accumulation. `[27]`

## Level 2 — Detail
Without buoyancy there's no upward plume, so a flame relaxes into a sphere or dome around the fuel. Because less oxygen is drawn in and radiation dominates heat loss, µg flames burn cooler — and cooler means less soot, so they're bluer and dimmer than Earth's bright yellow flames.
- No buoyant plume → flames become spherical/dome-shaped around the fuel source. `[14]`
- Lower temperature + less soot → dimmer, bluer flames (harder to detect). `[14, 27]`
- Radiation is the dominant heat-loss path, keeping µg flames cooler. `[14]`
- Visibility matters for fire safety: dim µg flames are a detection challenge. `[14, 28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
This is the single most counterintuitive fact for fire safety: *space fires are harder to see*. On Earth a fire announces itself with bright, flickering, sooty yellow flames and a rising plume. In microgravity it's a faint, dim, blue-ish sphere — cooler, less luminous, no plume. The China Space Station multi-jet study measured exactly this: pure methane burns dimmer (lower temperature), while nitrogen-diluted flames can actually *brighten* because soot gets confined and accumulates. For a smoke or optical detector tuned to Earth fires, a dim µg flame is a nightmare — which is precisely why detection (Module 3) is a dedicated challenge.
