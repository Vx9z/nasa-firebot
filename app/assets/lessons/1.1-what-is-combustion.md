# 1.1 What is combustion
**Module:** 1 — Combustion & Fire · **Prev:** [0.6](0.6-gravity-microgravity.md) · **Next:** [1.2](1.2-fire-tetrahedron.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Combustion is rapid, exothermic oxidation — the chemical reaction that powers fire.
- Combustion is the dominant energy source (transport, power, industry, heating). `[14]`
- It is a reaction between a fuel and an oxidizer (usually oxygen). `[14, 28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
Combustion takes a fuel (gas, liquid, or solid) and an oxidizer (usually oxygen from air) and turns them into products — mainly CO₂ and water — while releasing heat and light. It's "rapid oxidation": the same chemistry as rusting, but so fast it produces a flame.
- Oxidation: fuel + oxidizer → products (CO₂, H₂O) + heat; combustion is the fast, hot form. `[14]`
- Fuels may be gas, liquid, or solid — each combusts differently (jets, droplets, surfaces). 
- Combustion powers transportation, power generation, furnaces, and habitat heating. `[14]`
- "Combustion" is the underlying reaction; "fire" is its uncontrolled/flaming manifestation. 

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Combustion is everywhere because it's the most energy-dense way we release chemical energy. Refining metals, making plastics and ceramics, generating electricity, heating homes — all rely on it. In the space context, this matters twice over: (1) propulsion and life-support depend on controlled combustion, and (2) *uncontrolled* combustion is the fire hazard that drives this entire curriculum. The same reaction that powers a rocket is the one we must detect and suppress in a spacecraft cabin. Understanding combustion as *one reaction with many faces* is the thread that connects every later module.
