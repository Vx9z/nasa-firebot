# 1.3 Flame anatomy & types
**Module:** 1 — Combustion & Fire · **Prev:** [1.2](1.2-fire-tetrahedron.md) · **Next:** [1.4](1.4-ignition-flammability.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
A flame has distinct zones and comes in two main types: diffusion and premixed.
- Zones: preheat → reaction → post-flame. 
- Diffusion flames (fuel & oxidizer separate) vs. premixed (mixed beforehand). `[14]`
- Laminar vs. turbulent; buoyancy strongly shapes flame geometry on Earth. `[14]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A flame is a structured region, not a uniform blob. Fuel enters, heats up (preheat zone), reacts in a thin luminous zone, and products cool downstream (post-flame). In a diffusion flame (a candle), fuel and air meet only at the reaction sheet. In a premixed flame (a bunsen burner), they're mixed first.
- Preheat zone (fuel heated/evaporated), reaction zone (luminous), post-flame (products cool). 
- Diffusion flame: fuel and oxidizer meet at a thin reaction sheet (candle-like). `[14]`
- Premixed flame: fuel + oxidizer pre-mixed (bunsen burner); has a defined flame speed. 
- In µg, flames tend laminar (no buoyancy-driven turbulence) and become spherical. `[14]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The diffusion-vs-premixed distinction matters enormously for fire safety. A candle, a jet flame, and a burning solid surface are all *diffusion* flames — the burning rate is limited by how fast fuel and oxygen can diffuse together, not by chemical speed. That's why microgravity changes them so dramatically: without buoyancy pulling fresh air in, diffusion slows, flames shrink, dim, and reshape into spheres (Module 2). Premixed flames, by contrast, have an intrinsic "flame speed" and are how explosions and some engine combustion behave. Knowing which type you're dealing with tells you how a fire will grow, spread, and respond to suppression.
