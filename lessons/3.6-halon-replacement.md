# 3.6 Halon replacement
**Module:** 3 — Fire Detection & Suppression · **Prev:** [3.5](3.5-extinguishment-effectiveness.md) · **Next:** [4.1](4.1-material-flammability.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
The search for a safer extinguishing agent to replace ozone-depleting Halons.
- Halon replacements should have low ozone-depletion & global-warming potential, low toxicity, and be "drop-in." `[14]`
- Water mist is one promising alternative under investigation. `[14]`

## Level 2 — Detail
Halons were the gold standard for spacecraft suppression — effective, clean, low-toxicity at suppressing concentrations. But they destroy stratospheric ozone, so they're being phased out. The replacement must be just as good on every axis: effective, non-toxic, environmentally safe, and ideally a "drop-in" (same plumbing).
- Criteria for replacements: low ozone-depletion potential, low global-warming potential, low toxicity, "drop-in" (similar vapor pressure & viscosity). `[14]`
- Water mist (very fine droplets) is a promising old-tech/new-use alternative. `[14]`
- Motivation: Halons deplete stratospheric ozone, so they're being phased out. 

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The Halon problem is a perfect microcosm of real-world engineering: a technically excellent solution that turned out to have an unacceptable side effect. Halon 1301 breaks the flame's chain reaction at low concentration, is clean, and doesn't displace oxygen — ideal for a sealed cabin. But its bromine content destroys ozone, so the Montreal Protocol forced a phase-out. The hunt for a replacement weighs four things at once: environmental impact (low ODP and GWP), toxicity, effectiveness, and drop-in compatibility. Water mist — an old technology repurposed as ultra-fine droplets — is a front-runner because it cools, displaces oxygen, and is harmless to breathe in controlled amounts. It's a reminder that fire safety is never just about fire — it's about the whole system, including the planet.
