# 1.4 Ignition & flammability limits
**Module:** 1 — Combustion & Fire · **Prev:** [1.3](1.3-flame-anatomy-types.md) · **Next:** [1.5](1.5-extinction-blowoff.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
A fuel only burns within a range of conditions — its flammability limits.
- Flammability has lower/upper concentration limits (LFL/UFL). `[28, 36]`
- Ignition requires reaching the ignition temperature; materials vary widely. `[28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A fuel-air mixture only burns if the fuel fraction is within a certain window. Too lean (below the lower flammability limit) and there's not enough fuel; too rich (above the upper limit) and there's not enough oxygen. Ignition also needs enough heat to reach the material's ignition temperature.
- Lower flammability limit (LFL) = too lean to burn; upper (UFL) = too rich. 
- Ignition temperature = minimum to initiate; piloted vs. autoignition differ. 
- Oxygen-enriched atmospheres widen the flammable range → higher fire risk. `[22]`
- In µg, reduced convective cooling means less heat is carried away, aiding ignition of some materials. `[14, 28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Flammability limits are *the* number fire safety engineers live by — they define what's safe to have in a cabin. And microgravity quietly reshapes them: without buoyant convection to carry heat away, a material that self-extinguishes on Earth can keep burning in space (or vice-versa). FLARE and SIBAL (Module 2) exist precisely to measure these limits at reduced gravity, because the terrestrial "worst-case" test isn't always the real worst case in orbit. This is why flammability testing is a dedicated chapter of spacecraft fire safety (Module 4).

**Q1.** A mixture with too little fuel to burn is…

- b) below the LFL

- a) harder to ignite
