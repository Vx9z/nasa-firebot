# 3.3 Diagnostics & sensors
**Module:** 3 — Fire Detection & Suppression · **Prev:** [3.2](3.2-smoke-detection.md) · **Next:** [3.4](3.4-suppression-agents.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Measuring a flame without disturbing it — the science of combustion diagnostics.
- Diagnostics favor non-perturbing, rapid measurements (appearance, velocity, temperature, species). `[05, 14]`
- Two-color broadband pyrometry measures flame temperature. `[52]`

## Level 2 — Detail
To understand a flame you must measure it — but touching it changes it. So combustion science uses non-perturbing, usually optical, diagnostics: imaging the flame, measuring its temperature, velocity, and chemical species — all without contact. In microgravity this is even harder, because the flames are faint.
- Needed: non-perturbing, rapid measurement of flame appearance, velocity, temperature, chemical species. `[05, 14]`
- Image enhancement is required because µg flames are barely visible/weakly radiant. `[14]`
- Line-absorption spectroscopy (developed for µg) became an industrial smoke analyzer. `[14]`
- Two-color pyrometry is a non-contact way to measure flame temperature. `[52]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Diagnostics are the unsung hero of this whole field — you can't validate a model or a detector without good measurements. And microgravity forced innovation: faint, dim flames needed image enhancement; drop-tower and space experiments needed small, rugged, fast sensors that survive violent deceleration. The payoff was real — a line-absorption-spectroscopy instrument developed for microgravity species measurement later became a commercial on-line smoke analyzer. Two-color pyrometry measures temperature by comparing emission at two wavelengths, sidestepping the need for contact. Every Saffire, FLEX, and ACME result depends on these techniques — they're how "we think flames do X" becomes "we measured X."
