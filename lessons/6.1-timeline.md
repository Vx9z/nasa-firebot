## Level 1 — Overview
How the field evolved — from the first space flammability test to today.
- 1974 — Skylab M-479: first zero-gravity flammability experiment. `[01]`
- 2016–2024 — Saffire I–VI large-scale fire tests. `[39–47, 56]`

## Level 2 — Detail
The field's history is a steady climb in capability: early single experiments gave way to workshop-era fundamentals, then dedicated ISS campaigns (FLEX, ACME, BASS, SoFIE), and finally Saffire's large-scale tests and modern international work.
- 1974 — Skylab M-479: first zero-gravity flammability experiment. `[01]`
- 1993–2003 — 2nd–7th International Microgravity Combustion Workshops. `[08–13]`
- 1996 — TM-107353 defines the field's four application areas. `[14]`
- 2009–2015 — FLEX droplet combustion. `[18, 19, 20]`
- 2014–2021 — BASS/BASS-II solid combustion. `[31, 32]`
- 2019–2025 — SoFIE solid fuel. `[37, 64]`
- 2016–2024 — Saffire I–VI large-scale fire tests. `[39–47, 56]`
- 2021–2022 — ACME gaseous flames. `[23, 24]`
- 2026 — China Space Station multi-jet flames; droplet-flame & robotic-firefighting work. `[27, 65, 66]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Read this timeline and you see a story: **fundamental science → applied safety → prediction.** Skylab's 1974 test asked "does it even burn in space?" The 1990s workshops built the fundamental understanding. The 2000s–2010s ISS campaigns (FLEX droplets, ACME gases, BASS/SoFIE solids) dissected each combustion mode. Saffire then scaled it up to realistic fires, and the 2020s added predictive models and international data (China's station). And through it all, one motivation never changed: *fire safety for humans in space*. Every era built on the last — which is exactly the bottom-up structure of this entire curriculum.
