# 5.1 Spacecraft fire scenarios
**Module:** 5 — Spacecraft Fire Safety & Exploration · **Prev:** [4.4](4.4-flammability-testing-standards.md) · **Next:** [5.2](5.2-fire-safety-systems.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
The specific ways fire threatens a spacecraft — often differently than on Earth.
- Thermally-stressed components overheat (reduced air cooling in µg). `[14, 28]`
- Aerosol clouds / fluid leaks persist (slow settling); smoldering initiates readily. `[14, 28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A spacecraft fire starts differently than a house fire. Because there's no natural convection, hot components aren't cooled by rising air — so they overheat more easily. Leaked fluids and aerosols don't settle or disperse; they hang around as a fire hazard. And smoldering (Lesson 2.7) can begin with no flame at all.
- Overheating: near-absence of natural convection greatly reduces air cooling of hot components. `[14, 28]`
- Aerosol/fluid leaks persist as hazards because settling/dispersion are very slow in µg. `[14, 28]`
- Smoldering (low-temperature, flameless) initiates readily without natural convection. `[14, 28]`
- Oxygen-enriched atmospheres (e.g., O₂ storage) raise fire risk. `[22]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Every spacecraft fire scenario traces back to microgravity physics (Module 2). No convection → no passive cooling → components run hotter. No buoyancy → leaks and aerosols don't settle → a spilled liquid becomes a persistent vapor cloud. No buoyant plume → fires smolder invisibly instead of flaring. And if the environment is oxygen-enriched — a real risk around O₂ storage or life-support systems — everything ignites more easily and burns hotter. Understanding these scenarios is why spacecraft fire safety can't just borrow Earth's rulebook; it's a distinct problem with its own failure modes.
