# 4.2 Toxic & hazardous products
**Module:** 4 — Materials, Toxicity & Standards · **Prev:** [4.1](4.1-material-flammability.md) · **Next:** [4.3](4.3-combustion-products-acid-gases.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
What burning materials release, and why it's dangerous beyond the flames.
- PTFE wire insulation degrades to toxic gases + ultrafine (lung-hazardous) particles. `[14]`
- HCl (hydrogen chloride) uptake onto interior spacecraft materials is modeled. `[51]`

## Level 2 — Detail
The fire itself isn't the only threat — the *smoke and gases* are often the real killer. Burning polymers release acids, ammonia, carbon monoxide, and ultrafine particles. In a sealed cabin, these accumulate with nowhere to go.
- Polymers in spacecraft may generate acids, ammonia, and other toxic products. `[28]`
- Overpressure/explosions from igniting reactive gaseous products are an additional injury risk. `[28]`
- Exposure limits should reflect mental acuity & judgment (the psychology of escape), not just toxicity. `[28]`
- HCl is a major acid-gas product that interacts with surfaces. `[51]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Toxicity is the reason "fire safety" in a spacecraft is really about *air quality under fire*. The crew can't evacuate, so the cabin fills with whatever the fire releases. Carbon monoxide is the classic silent killer (odorless, displaces oxygen in the blood), but spacecraft materials add their own threats — PTFE insulation releases hydrogen fluoride and ultrafine particles, and other polymers release acids and ammonia. Even the *exposure standard* is different: because a fire impairs thinking, limits are set by mental acuity and the "psychology of escape," not just raw toxicity. Post-fire, acid gases like HCl linger and react with surfaces — which is why cleanup (Lesson 4.3) is its own problem.
