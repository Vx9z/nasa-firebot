# 3.1 Fire signatures
**Module:** 3 — Fire Detection & Suppression · **Prev:** [2.7](2.7-smoldering.md) · **Next:** [3.2](3.2-smoke-detection.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Fires announce themselves through measurable "signatures" — heat, smoke, light, and gas.
- Signatures classify into heat, smoke, light (radiation), and gas. `[28]`
- Detectors respond to these environmental departures from normal conditions. `[28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A fire detector can't "see" fire directly — it senses the side-effects: a temperature rise (heat), particles (smoke), flame radiation (light), or combustion gases (CO, CO₂). Each signature is a signal, and each detector type listens for one (or a few) of them.
- Signatures: heat (temperature rise), smoke (particles), light (flame radiation), gas (CO, CO₂). `[28]`
- Each detector type senses one (or a few) signatures — each has strengths/weaknesses. `[28]`
- Detector selection must also account for how fast the signature reaches the sensor (transport). `[28]`
- In µg, dim/flameless fires make "light" and "heat" signatures weak → detection is harder. `[14, 28]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
The signature concept is the *lingua franca* of fire detection — and it's why space fires are hard. On Earth, a fire is loud in all four signatures: bright light, hot air, thick smoke, gases. In microgravity, a fire can be a faint blue sphere (weak light), cooler (weak heat), with smoke that doesn't rise (weak transport), leaving mostly smoke and gas as reliable signals. So spacecraft detectors lean on smoke and gas, and the whole field of spacecraft detection (smoke particulate characterization, next lesson) exists to squeeze the most signal out of a *weaker* fire. Choosing the right signature — and knowing its limits — is the first decision in fire safety.
