# 4.4 Flammability testing & standards
**Module:** 4 — Materials, Toxicity & Standards · **Prev:** [4.3](4.3-combustion-products-acid-gases.md) · **Next:** [5.1](5.1-spacecraft-fire-scenarios.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
How we predict full-scale fire behavior from small tests — and set the rules.
- Historically flammability tests were empirical; new bench-scale tests predict full-scale behavior. `[28]`
- New tests evaluate ignitability, flame spread, and heat & product release. `[28]`

## Level 2 — Detail
You can't burn a whole spacecraft to test it, so you test small samples and *predict* the big picture. Early flammability tests were crude rules of thumb; modern bench-scale tests are grounded in real fire science and measure specific properties — how easily something ignites, how fast flame spreads, how much heat and product it releases.
- Older tests were empirical, not rooted in fire science. `[28]`
- New-generation bench-scale tests are based on physical/chemical phenomena for systematic prediction. `[28]`
- They evaluate ignitability, flame spread, and heat & product release. `[28]`
- SIBAL (inflammability boundary) and FLARE (flammability limits) refine material testing in µg. `[15, 36]`
- Large-scale spacecraft flammability tests inform standards. `[43]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Standards are the quiet backbone of fire safety — they decide what materials are *allowed* on a spacecraft. The problem: most standards were built from Earth (1-g) tests, and microgravity breaks the assumptions. FLARE and SIBAL exist precisely because the terrestrial "worst-case" test isn't always the real worst case in orbit — some materials that pass on Earth behave worse in µg. So the push is toward tests that predict full-scale, *microgravity* behavior from bench-scale data. Saffire's large-scale tests then validate those predictions, closing the loop: small test → model → full-scale check → standard. That's how a material gets certified safe to fly.
