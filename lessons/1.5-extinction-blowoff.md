# 1.5 Extinction & blowoff
**Module:** 1 — Combustion & Fire · **Prev:** [1.4](1.4-ignition-flammability.md) · **Next:** [1.6](1.6-soot-combustion-products.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Flames go out in a few distinct ways — blowoff, radiative loss, and dilution.
- Blowoff: flame is pushed off when flow speed exceeds burning capability. `[35]`
- Radiant extinction: heat loss (radiation) can extinguish weak flames. `[14]`
- Oxygen concentration directly affects blowoff limits. `[35]`

## Level 2 — Detail
A flame survives only if it can keep itself going. If a flow pushes the flame away faster than it can propagate back toward the fuel, it blows off. If it radiates away more heat than it generates, it dies by radiative extinction. And if you dilute the oxygen enough, combustion can't continue.
- Blowoff happens when the flow velocity exceeds the flame's ability to propagate back toward the fuel. `[35]`
- Radiative extinction: a weak flame radiates away more heat than it releases → dies. `[14]`
- Dilution/inerting (adding N₂, CO₂) drops oxygen below the flammability limit → extinction. `[28]`
- In µg, radiative loss is a dominant extinction mechanism (no convective cooling to help). `[14]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Extinction is the *flip side* of ignition, and it's just as important for safety. Blowoff explains why a fire can be "blown out" and why ventilation matters. Radiative extinction is the reason microgravity flames are fragile — they can't shed heat by convection, so radiation (∝ T⁴) bleeds them dry, especially sooty flames. And dilution/inerting is the basis of CO₂ and N₂ suppression (Module 3). Understanding extinction tells you not just *how fires start* but *how to reliably stop them* — and why the same fire behaves so differently in space.
