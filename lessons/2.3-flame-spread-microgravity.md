# 2.3 Flame spread in microgravity
**Module:** 2 — Microgravity Combustion · **Prev:** [2.2](2.2-flame-shape-color-temperature.md) · **Next:** [2.4](2.4-droplet-combustion.md)

## Level 1 — Overview
Fire moves across solid surfaces in two ways, and microgravity can make it *faster*, not slower.
- Concurrent (flow-aligned) vs. opposed (against-flow) spread. `[33, 49]`
- Some materials spread as fast or faster in µg than on Earth. `[14, 33]`

## Level 2 — Detail
"Flame spread" is how a flame advances along a solid fuel. It can move *with* the airflow (concurrent, wind-aided) or *against* it (opposed). The intuition that "no gravity = slower fire" is wrong for some materials — removing buoyant heat loss can actually accelerate spread.
- Concurrent (wind-aided) vs. opposed (against the flow) spread have different physics. `[33, 49]`
- No buoyant heat loss in µg can accelerate spread for some materials. `[14, 33]`
- The 1-g "worst case" assumption for material testing is not always valid. `[14, 28]`
- Thickness, preheat, and oxygen level all shift µg spread rates. `[33, 34]`

## Level 3 — Deep dive
Flame spread is where the "space fires are slow" myth breaks down — and it has real safety consequences. On Earth, buoyancy draws cool air in and carries heat up and away, which can *slow* spread. In microgravity, that heat-loss pathway disappears, so for certain materials (thin fuels, certain orientations) the flame can spread as fast as — or faster than — on the ground. This is why NASA's SIBAL and FLARE experiments exist: to map the flammability *boundary* in reduced gravity, because the terrestrial "worst case" test can be misleading. The practical lesson: you can't assume Earth flammability data transfers to a spacecraft.
